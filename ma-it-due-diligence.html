The problem

What exactly are you buying?

A business can look healthy in the accounts and be held together by a server that went out of support years ago, software nobody is licensed for and one long-serving employee who is the only person who understands how it all works.

None of that appears in a financial model. All of it becomes the buyer's cost on completion.

In our experience, a buyer's technology diligence comes down to four questions: is it secure, is it documented and licensed, does it depend on one person, and is the spending efficient and correctly treated? We answer all four with evidence.

What it's worth

Findings you can negotiate with

A diligence report is only useful if it changes a decision. Ours is written to be used at the negotiating table.

  • For buyers: price the real cost of the technology you are inheriting, and avoid surprises after completion.
  • For sellers: find and fix the issues first, so the buyer's adviser has nothing to chip the price with.
  • For investors: an independent view of whether the technology can support the growth plan.
Is it right for you?

An honest fit check

A good fit if

  • You are acquiring or investing in a business
  • You plan to sell within one to three years and want to be ready
  • The target relies heavily on its systems to operate
  • Your advisers want specialist technology input

Probably not the right fit if

  • The deal has already completed. An IT strategy review is the better starting point
  • You need legal or financial due diligence. We complement those advisers and do not replace them
Questions owners ask

Frequently asked questions

What is IT due diligence?

It is an independent assessment of a company's technology carried out before a merger, acquisition or investment. It covers systems, costs, contracts, licensing, security, people and how difficult integration will be.

What is the difference between IT due diligence and technology due diligence?

They are used interchangeably for most businesses. Technology due diligence sometimes implies a deeper look at software products a company has built. For businesses that use technology and do not sell it, they are the same exercise.

What does IT due diligence look for?

Security record and incident history, documentation and licence compliance, key-person dependency and the quality of technology spending, along with the likely cost of integration.

We are selling. How does this help us?

A seller who commissions the review first can fix issues on their own timetable and present clean evidence. That supports the valuation and shortens the buyer's process. Our guide to realising value at exit explains why it matters.
