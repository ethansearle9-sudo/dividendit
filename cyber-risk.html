The cyber risk facing small and medium businesses: what the data says

Nearly a third of businesses suffered a successful cyber attack in the past year. This is not a large-company problem, and the most reliable recent research makes that very clear. Here is what it shows, without the scare tactics.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·7 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. The scale of the problem
  2. Why smaller businesses are the target
  3. How attackers actually get in
  4. The consequences go well beyond the clean-up
  5. What good looks like
  6. The bottom line

The scale of the problem

For years, cyber security was treated as something for banks and governments to worry about. The data has comprehensively overturned that view.

  • 29% of businesses suffered at least one successful cyber attack in the past twelve months, and those that were hit were hit four times on average, according to the Hiscox Cyber Readiness Report 2026.
  • The UK Government's 2025 survey found that 43% of businesses identified a breach or attack in the previous year, rising to 67% of medium-sized businesses.

The two surveys measure slightly different things, successful attacks in one and all identified breaches or attacks in the other, but they point the same way. For a business of any size, being targeted is now routine. The useful question is no longer whether it will happen. It is whether you will notice, and how ready you will be.

Why smaller businesses are the target

Attackers go where the defences are weakest and the money still moves. Large enterprises have invested heavily in security teams and round-the-clock monitoring. Smaller businesses usually have limited IT budgets, no dedicated security staff and nobody watching outside office hours, and they still pay suppliers and hold customer data.

Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report, the largest annual study of real incidents, shows the result:

  • Small and medium organisations accounted for roughly three times as many incidents as large ones in its dataset: 3,049 against 982.
  • 99% of breaches of smaller organisations were financially motivated. This is organised crime, not mischief.
  • Ransomware was present in 88% of breaches at smaller organisations, compared with 39% at large ones.

How attackers actually get in

The routes in are consistent year after year, and none of them is exotic.

RouteWhat happensWhat the data says
PhishingA convincing email leads someone to a fake login page or a malicious fileExperienced by 85% of UK businesses that identified a breach or attack
Stolen passwordsCredentials taken by phishing, or reused from another site, are simply used to log inThe most common hacking technique in breaches of smaller organisations
Unpatched systemsA known weakness in software or a firewall is exploited before the fix is appliedThe top technical root cause of ransomware attacks, at 32%
SuppliersAn attacker comes in through a trusted third party's access or softwareThird-party involvement in breaches doubled to 30%

Around 60% of breaches involve a human element: someone clicking, approving or being talked into something. That is not a criticism of staff. It is a reminder that controls have to work even when a busy person makes an ordinary mistake.

The most expensive version for smaller businesses is payment fraud by email. See how invoice fraud works and how to spot a phishing email.

The consequences go well beyond the clean-up

Hiscox asked businesses that had been attacked what it did to them. The answers are the part most relevant to a shareholder.

  • 30% reported a negative effect on financial performance, valuation or credit rating.
  • 32% said growth, expansion or new initiatives were delayed.
  • 29% lost business opportunities or partnerships.
  • 28% faced financial penalties, and 26% suffered negative publicity.
  • The average downtime after an incident was just under 33 hours.

The average cost of cyber incidents to a business over the year was around $52,000. For the full breakdown of where that money goes, and why the true figure is usually higher, see the true cost of a cyber attack.

What good looks like

The encouraging part of the data is how preventable most of this is. The routes in are well known, and a small number of well-run controls close them:

  • Multi-factor sign-in on every account
  • Protected email, and staff who know what a fraud attempt looks like
  • Managed detection and response on every device, with people watching
  • Updates applied everywhere, on time
  • Backups that are separate, protected and tested
  • A written plan for the first hour of an incident

None of that requires a large budget or an internal security team. Our owner's guide to cyber security for small business explains each one and how to decide whether to run them yourself or outsource them.

The bottom line

Over any meaningful period, a cyber attack on your business is a near certainty. Whether it becomes a non-event, a bad week or a threat to the value of what you have built depends on decisions made beforehand.

The cost of prevention is known and modest. The cost of an incident is neither. For owners who have spent years building something valuable, that is a straightforward calculation.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of small businesses experience a cyber attack?

It depends on the survey and the definition. Hiscox found 29% of businesses suffered a successful attack in the past twelve months. The UK Government found 43% of all businesses, and 67% of medium-sized businesses, identified a breach or attack in a year.

Why do hackers target small businesses?

Because smaller businesses typically have weaker defences and no round-the-clock monitoring, while still moving money and holding valuable data. Verizon found that 99% of breaches of smaller organisations were financially motivated.

What is the most common cyber attack on small businesses?

Phishing. It was experienced by 85% of UK businesses that identified a breach or attack, and it is the usual starting point for stolen passwords, invoice fraud and ransomware.
