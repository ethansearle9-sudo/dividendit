How to spot a phishing email: a guide for business teams

Phishing is the most common way into a business. It no longer looks like a badly spelled message from a prince. It looks like a shared document, a password expiry notice or an invoice from a supplier you use. Here is a ten-second check your team can use on every email that asks for something.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·8 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. Phishing has changed
  2. The ten-second check
  3. What phishing emails look like now
  4. What to do if someone clicks
  5. Building a team that catches it

Phishing has changed

The old advice was to look for spelling mistakes. That no longer works. Criminals use the same tools as everyone else to write clean, natural, well-formatted emails, often in your own industry's language.

Most phishing aimed at businesses today has one goal: to get someone to type their email password into a fake login page. Once they have it, they have the mailbox, and from there they can commit invoice fraud or move deeper into your systems. Many phishing emails have no attachment and nothing a virus scanner would catch. Just a link.

The ten-second check

Before acting on any email that asks you to click, log in, pay, or open something, run through five questions.

  1. Was I expecting this? An unexpected document, invoice, voicemail or delivery notice is the most common bait.
  2. Who really sent it? Look at the actual email address, not the display name. "Microsoft 365" can be sent from anything.
  3. Is it pushing me to act fast? Urgency, deadlines, account suspension and "the CEO needs this now" are pressure tactics.
  4. Where does the link actually go? Hover over it on a computer, or press and hold on a phone. If the web address is not the organisation it claims to be, stop.
  5. Is it asking me to log in, pay, or change payment details? These three requests deserve a second channel: open the website yourself, or phone the person on a number you already have.

The golden rule: never log in from a link in an email. Go to the site or app the way you normally would. If the request is real, it will be there.

What phishing emails look like now

The password expiry notice

"Your Microsoft 365 password expires today. Keep your current password." The button leads to a perfect copy of the Microsoft login page.

The shared document

"A file has been shared with you: Q3 Remittance.pdf". It may genuinely come from a supplier's real account, because their mailbox was compromised first. The link asks you to sign in to view it.

The invoice or payment change

A real-looking invoice, or a note that bank details have changed. Covered in detail in our invoice fraud guide.

The director request

"Are you at your desk? I need a quick favour." Followed by a request for gift cards or an urgent transfer, usually when the director is known to be away.

The QR code

An email or PDF with a QR code to "verify your account" or "view your payslip". Scanning moves you to your phone, where links are harder to inspect and company protections may not apply.

The fake multi-factor request

A run of sign-in approval prompts on your phone you did not trigger, sometimes followed by a call from "IT support" asking you to approve one. Never approve a sign-in you did not start.

What to do if someone clicks

The worst outcome is someone who clicks, realises, and says nothing because they are embarrassed. Make it easy and blame-free to speak up.

  1. Tell IT straight away, even if nothing seemed to happen.
  2. If a password was entered, change it immediately from a trusted device, and sign the account out everywhere.
  3. Check the account's sign-in methods and inbox rules for anything new.
  4. If a file was opened, disconnect the computer from the network and let your IT provider check it.

If it looks more serious than a single click, follow what to do in the first 24 hours.

Building a team that catches it

Technology filters out most phishing, but not all of it. What catches the rest is people who recognise the pattern and report it.

  • Short and regular beats long and annual. A few minutes of practical training each quarter keeps it fresh.
  • Practise with realistic simulations, and use the results to coach, not to shame.
  • A one-click report button in Outlook, so reporting is quicker than deleting.
  • Thank people who report, including false alarms. You want more reports, not fewer.
  • Directors go first. Senior people are targeted most and set the tone for everyone else.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if an email is fraudulent?

Check whether you were expecting it, look at the real sender address rather than the display name, be wary of urgency, hover over links to see where they really go, and treat any request to log in, pay or change payment details as something to verify through a second channel.

What should I do with a phishing email?

Do not click links or open attachments. Report it using your report button or by forwarding it to your IT provider, then delete it. If you already clicked or entered a password, tell IT immediately.

Can you get hacked just by opening a phishing email?

Opening an email on its own is very rarely enough today. The risk comes from clicking a link and entering details, opening an attachment, or approving a sign-in request. That said, report it so your provider can check whether others received it.

What is the most common type of phishing for businesses?

Credential phishing: emails that lead to a fake login page for Microsoft 365, Google or another service, designed to steal a password. The stolen mailbox is then used for invoice fraud or further phishing.
