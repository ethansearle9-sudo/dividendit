The true cost of a cyber attack on a business: beyond the headline figure

Cyber incidents cost the average business around $52,000 a year. That number is real, and it still understates the problem, because the largest costs are the ones that never appear on an invoice. Here are the five places the money goes.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·7 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. The headline figure is only the beginning
  2. Cost 1: Direct recovery
  3. Cost 2: Payment fraud
  4. Cost 3: Downtime and lost productivity
  5. Cost 4: Lost opportunities and penalties
  6. Cost 5: What it does to the value of the business
  7. The prevention calculation

The headline figure is only the beginning

The Hiscox Cyber Readiness Report 2026 puts the average cost of cyber incidents to a business at around $52,000 over twelve months. It is a useful anchor. It is also an average across thousands of businesses, many of which had a minor incident and a few of which had a catastrophic one.

For an owner, the more useful view is to look at the five categories of cost separately, because they behave very differently, and because the last one is the one that matters most when the business is valued.

Cost 1: Direct recovery

This is the visible bill: specialists to investigate and contain the incident, rebuilding systems, restoring data, replacing equipment and paying for legal and regulatory advice.

Where ransomware is involved, the numbers climb quickly. Across organisations of all sizes, Sophos found the average cost of recovering from a ransomware attack, before any ransom, was $1.53 million. Smaller businesses will usually be well below that, but the ratio to turnover can be just as painful.

The single largest factor in the size of this bill is whether there are clean, tested backups. See backup and disaster recovery.

Cost 2: Payment fraud

For most smaller businesses, this is the largest direct loss, and it often involves no damage to systems at all. An attacker gets into a mailbox, waits for a large payment and redirects it.

  • The FBI recorded $2.77 billion in reported losses to business email compromise in 2024, from more than 21,000 complaints.
  • 79% of organisations experienced attempted or actual payments fraud in 2024, and business email compromise was the most common route, according to the Association for Financial Professionals.
  • Getting the money back is becoming harder. Only 22% of organisations recovered 75% or more of the funds they lost, down from 41% the year before.

A single diverted supplier payment can exceed everything else on this page. See how invoice fraud works and how to stop it.

Cost 3: Downtime and lost productivity

When systems are down, people are paid to wait, orders are not shipped and invoices are not sent. Hiscox found the average downtime after an incident was just under 33 hours, most of a working week.

The cost of an hour depends entirely on your business, so work out your own:

A rough formula: (annual revenue ÷ working hours in a year) + (number of affected staff × their average hourly cost). Multiply by the hours you would be down. For a distribution or manufacturing business working to tight schedules, add any penalties for late delivery.

Then ask a harder question: how many hours would it actually take to restore your systems today? If nobody can answer, that is the first thing to find out.

Cost 4: Lost opportunities and penalties

The effects carry on after systems are restored. Among businesses that had been attacked, Hiscox found:

  • 32% delayed growth, expansion or new business initiatives
  • 31% faced higher costs for staff or outside expertise
  • 29% lost business opportunities or partnerships
  • 28% faced financial penalties
  • 26% suffered negative publicity

In sectors built on trust, such as financial services and professional advice, clients do not easily forgive a firm that loses control of their information. That loss rarely appears in any incident estimate and can outlast all the others.

Cost 5: What it does to the value of the business

This is the cost most owners never calculate. In the same Hiscox research, 30% of businesses that were attacked reported a negative effect on their financial performance, valuation or credit rating.

The mechanics are simple. A business is valued on a multiple of its earnings. An incident hits both halves of that equation:

  • Earnings fall in the year of the incident, through every cost above.
  • The multiple can fall too. A buyer who finds a recent breach, or weak controls, prices the risk in: a lower offer, a retention held back, or a deal that stalls in due diligence.

If the incident lands in the year a buyer bases their offer on, a $50,000 hit to earnings is not a $50,000 problem. At a five times multiple it can mean a quarter of a million off the price, before any discount for risk. Our value calculator shows the effect for your own numbers.

The prevention calculation

CostHow it behaves
Direct recoveryTens of thousands for a contained incident, far more with ransomware and no usable backups
Payment fraudThe size of your largest supplier payment, and increasingly hard to recover
DowntimeYour hourly cost multiplied by days, not hours
Lost opportunitiesDelayed plans, lost customers and penalties, lasting months
Business valueLost earnings multiplied at exit, plus any discount for risk

Against that, the cost of properly managed security is known in advance, fixed each month and a small fraction of any one of these lines. The business case is not a matter of opinion. It is arithmetic.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a cyber attack cost a small business?

Hiscox puts the average cost of cyber incidents to a business at around $52,000 a year, but the range is very wide. A contained incident may cost a few thousand. A diverted payment or a ransomware attack without usable backups can cost many times the average.

What is the biggest cost of a cyber attack?

For smaller businesses the largest direct loss is usually payment fraud, where a genuine payment is redirected to a criminal. The largest indirect cost is often the effect on business value, through lost earnings and a lower valuation multiple.

Does cyber insurance cover the cost of an attack?

It can cover much of the direct cost, including incident response, restoration and business interruption, subject to the policy terms. Payment fraud is often excluded or covered at a lower limit. See our guide to cyber insurance.
