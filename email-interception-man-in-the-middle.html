Email interception and man-in-the-middle attacks, in plain English

A man-in-the-middle attack is exactly what it sounds like: someone quietly placing themselves between you and the person you are dealing with, reading and changing what passes between you. Here is how it happens to businesses, and what stops it.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·8 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. What "man in the middle" means
  2. The three ways it happens to businesses
  3. Why multi-factor sign-in is essential but not always enough
  4. How to protect the business
  5. Signs someone is already in the middle

What "man in the middle" means

Imagine a postman who opens your letters, reads them, changes the bank details on the invoice, reseals the envelope and delivers it. Neither you nor the recipient can tell anything happened. That is a man-in-the-middle attack.

In business, the "letters" are emails, logins and payment instructions. The attacker's aim is almost always money: redirect a payment, or steal the access that lets them redirect one later.

The three ways it happens to businesses

1. Inside a compromised mailbox

The most common version is not technical at all. An attacker steals a password, gets into a mailbox, and becomes the "man in the middle" of every conversation in it. They can read, reply, hide messages and change attachments. Some security people call this "man in the mailbox". It is the engine behind most invoice fraud.

2. A lookalike domain slipped into the thread

Having read a conversation, the attacker copies it and continues it from a near-identical address: dividendit.co instead of dividendit.com, or an "rn" that looks like an "m". Both sides think they are talking to each other. They are both talking to the criminal.

3. Interception of the login itself

This is the more technical version. The victim clicks a phishing link that leads to a proxy sitting between them and the real Microsoft or Google login page. They enter their password and approve their multi-factor prompt as normal, and the proxy captures the session that results. The attacker can then use the account without needing the password or the second factor again. This is often called an adversary-in-the-middle attack.

Public and poorly secured WiFi can also be used to intercept traffic, although widespread encryption has made that much less common than it used to be.

Why multi-factor sign-in is essential but not always enough

Multi-factor sign-in stops the vast majority of password attacks and is the first thing every business should have. But the third technique above is designed specifically to get around the most common forms of it, such as text codes and push approvals.

The answer is not to abandon it. It is to strengthen it:

  • Phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys or physical security keys for directors, finance and administrators. They will not work on a fake login page.
  • Conditional access rules that only allow sign-in from managed company devices or expected locations, so a stolen session is useless from the attacker's machine.
  • Monitoring for risky sign-ins, such as one account appearing in two countries at once, with someone ready to revoke the session within minutes.

We cover this in more depth on our identity and access management page.

How to protect the business

  • Verify every payment change by phone, on a number you already had. This one habit defeats all three techniques.
  • Use strong multi-factor sign-in, with phishing-resistant methods for high-risk people and conditional access for everyone.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your domain and move DMARC to enforcement, so criminals cannot send email as your exact domain.
  • Register the obvious lookalikes of your domain and monitor for new ones.
  • Flag external email so a message from outside the business is clearly marked, even if the name looks familiar.
  • Watch for inbox rules, forwarding and unusual sign-ins, with alerts that reach someone who acts on them, day and night.
  • Use a company VPN or your phone's hotspot rather than public WiFi for anything sensitive.

Signs someone is already in the middle

  • A supplier or customer says they never received an email you sent, or received one you did not.
  • Replies arrive from an address that is one character different from the real one.
  • Someone sees a multi-factor prompt they did not trigger, or sign-ins from places they have not been.
  • Inbox rules or forwarding appear that nobody set up.

Frequently asked questions

What is a man-in-the-middle attack in simple terms?

It is when an attacker secretly places themselves between two parties who think they are talking directly to each other, so they can read, change or redirect what passes between them, such as emails, logins or payment instructions.

Can multi-factor authentication stop man-in-the-middle attacks?

Basic multi-factor sign-in, such as text codes or push approvals, can be bypassed by adversary-in-the-middle phishing. Phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys and security keys, combined with conditional access rules, are much more effective.

How do criminals intercept business emails?

Most often by stealing a password and getting into a real mailbox, then watching conversations. Sometimes they continue a stolen conversation from a lookalike domain. Less often, they intercept a login session through a phishing proxy.

Is public WiFi safe for business email?

It is much safer than it once was because most connections are encrypted, but it is still wise to use a company VPN or your phone's hotspot for anything sensitive, and never to ignore certificate or security warnings.
