Outsourced IT Support That Builds Value: The Business Owner's Guide

Every technology decision your business makes gets paid for twice: once as a cost, and again in what your business is worth. This guide shows owners and shareholders how to make those decisions deliberately, using outsourced IT support and cybersecurity to protect value, build it, and stop it leaking away by default.

Chapter one

The decision nobody connects to value.

Around 70% of business owners who set out to sell never manage it. Decades of work, risk and missed evenings that never turn into realised value for the person who built the business.

It rarely happens in one dramatic moment. Value leaks. Earnings come in smaller than they should. A buyer finds a risk and prices it against you. The business can't run without its owner. And underneath all of it sit technology decisions nobody ever connected to the number that matters.

Here is a conversation I have had more times than I can count. A finance director tells me: "I want to move everything to the cloud, because it's cheaper and I don't have to worry about it." It sounds sensible. For that business, neither half was true. Had they followed the default advice, they would have paid for it every month for seven years, and paid again in the price they got when they sold.

Most owners treat IT as a utility bill: something to minimise and hand to someone else. But every technology decision lands in one of two places, your earnings or your risk. Those are exactly the two things a buyer uses to decide what your business is worth.

Chapter two

The value equation: every decision, twice.

When a business is sold, the mechanics are simple: enterprise value = EBITDA × a multiple. Your earnings, times a number that reflects your sector, growth and risk.

In the UK & Ireland lower mid-market, that multiple currently averages around 5.3×, with typical SME deals between roughly 4× and 12×. Smaller businesses carry a discount for concentration risk and dependence on the owner.

Your EBITDA€500,000
At a 4× multiple€2,000,000
At a 6× multiple€3,000,000
The difference: same business, better positioned€1,000,000
Multiple benchmarks: Dealsuite UK&I M&A Monitor H1 2025; UK SME valuation guides 2025–26. Illustrative worked example.

The best owners I have worked with made every technology decision twice. A medical device distributor I advised would sit with us and look at each recommendation first as a saving, then multiply that saving by their exit multiple. To them, a €5,000 a year saving was never a €5,000 decision: at a 6× multiple it was worth €30,000 at sale. They sold about a year later.

Two rules follow, and the rest of this guide hangs off them:

  • Every euro of recurring cost is multiplied. Cut it, and you add it to the business several times over at sale. Waste it, and you lose it several times over.
  • Every risk your systems carry is priced. Buyers don't just buy your profit and loss. They buy your risk, and unmanaged technology risk shows up as a lower multiple, a price chip, or a deal that dies in diligence.

That is why IT is a value lever, not a utility. It sits on both sides of the equation.

Chapter three

Workloads: IT explained without the jargon.

You don't need to understand servers to make good technology decisions. You need one idea: workloads. A workload is a job your technology does for the business. Running your accounting system is a workload. Email is a workload. Your warehouse system, your client files, your phones and, increasingly, your AI tools are all workloads.

Most businesses have four layers of them:

  • The foundation: devices, network, internet, security, sign-in. This is what a traditional IT company manages. It has to just work, and nobody should notice it.
  • Your line-of-business systems: the software your business actually runs on, from Sage or Syspro to a practice management or stock system. This is where your operations live.
  • Your data: customers, pricing, history, know-how. This is much of the intellectual capital a buyer is paying for.
  • AI on top: tools that use your data to do work faster. Treat AI as just another workload: it needs the same access controls and the same question of where it runs and what it costs.

The industry tends to sell each layer separately, with a different vendor for each and a reason to upgrade every year. That is how businesses end up with technology for technology's sake: tools bought because they were new, not because they moved a number.

The better question for every workload is simple: what does this do for earnings or risk, and where should it run to do that at the lowest total cost? Asked workload by workload, that question is where most of the value in this guide comes from.

Chapter four · Lever one

Own vs rent: the compute decision nobody makes deliberately.

For a decade, the default answer to almost every question has been "move it to the cloud." For workloads that genuinely vary, such as a fast-growing online product or a seasonal spike, that is the right answer. Elastic capacity is what public cloud is for.

Most established businesses don't run workloads like that. Their core systems do roughly the same work every day. Renting that capacity month after month is one of the quietest ways a business erodes its own earnings, because rented compute is expensed: it comes straight off EBITDA every month. Owned equipment is capitalised: it sits on the balance sheet and depreciates, below the EBITDA line.

A distribution group I worked with after a management buyout had to build its IT from scratch. The instruction was to put everything in the cloud. Instead, we compared the two options for their core business system:

Buy a server with a five-year warranty~€7,500 once
Rent the same capacity in the cloud~€1,250 a month
Payback on owning6 months
Actual working life of the server7 years
Client example, converted from rand at roughly R20 to the euro. Support, backup and security were needed either way. Hosting the server in a data centre gave the branches the same uptime as the cloud.

Same technical outcome. Better capital allocation. The one thing they gave up was the ability to scale up overnight, which a business that owns its market and grows steadily doesn't need. The group was later acquired. Had they followed the default, that value would have leaked out every month for seven years.

The market is noticing. In an IDC survey, 86% of CIOs planned to move at least some workloads back from public cloud in 2025, though only about 8% plan a full exit. The answer isn't "cloud or no cloud." It's hybrid: rent what genuinely needs to flex, own what runs steady, and decide workload by workload with the EBITDA effect on the table.

Chapter five · Lever two

Security is value protection, priced in euros, not fear.

Nearly a third of businesses suffered a successful cyber attack in the past twelve months, and cyber incidents cost the average business around $52,000 a year. That is only the visible part. Three in ten of the businesses that were hit said it damaged their financial performance, valuation or credit rating.

The attacks that hurt owners most are rarely sophisticated. An email that looks like it comes from a supplier asks for new bank details. A finance team member pays a real-looking invoice. A mailbox is quietly taken over and used to redirect payments. The biggest weakness sits between the keyboard and the chair, and criminals know it.

Here is the honest part most providers won't say: everyone sells you the promise that nothing will go wrong, and something always will. What protects your value is how fast it's spotted, how well it's contained, and how calmly it's fixed. Ask any provider to show you that, not promise it.

For a shareholder, the sharper point is what security does to the equation. A breach in the year before a sale can chip the price, stretch out diligence or kill the deal. A clean, evidenced security record is one of the few things that makes a buyer's diligence faster.

Most small business risk is covered by a handful of well-run controls: monitoring and response around the clock, protected email, multi-factor sign-in everywhere, backups that are actually tested, and staff who can spot the fraud attempt. Tooling no 30-person business could justify buying alone is exactly what a good outsourced partner spreads across its clients.

Chapter six · Lever three

Spend on your people, not your servers.

Ask where the value in your business is created, then check where your technology budget goes. They are often in different places.

A wealth management firm I worked with makes its money through advisers sitting with clients around the world, building trust over years. Yet its budget went on running every system in the public cloud, expensed every month. Those systems could go down for a few hours without crippling the business. A compromised adviser's laptop, or a client's money redirected by a fake email, could do real damage. The fix was to stop paying for capacity it didn't need and put that money into protecting the people who create the value.

The same logic applies to everyday friction. Fifteen minutes a day of slow systems, login problems and waiting per person is roughly 60 hours a year each. Across a 40-person business, that is more than one full-time salary paid for waiting.

And it applies to you. Every IT problem that reaches the owner's desk takes an hour from the work only you can do. The goal of good outsourced IT support is that technology drops off your list entirely. As one client put it: "IT used to be a top-five problem for me. Now I don't even think about it."

Chapter seven · Lever four

Exit readiness: what buyers actually look at.

Around 78% of owners have no formal transition team, which usually means technology diligence is prepared for the first time when a buyer is already at the table. That is the worst moment to find the gaps.

Buyers' technology diligence usually checks four things: security record and incident history, documentation and licence compliance, key-person dependency (does the IT knowledge live in one head?), and cost quality (is spend efficient, predictable and treated correctly in the accounts?).

Cost quality is where the biggest wins hide. A manufacturer I worked with had its entire IT spend reviewed line by line, with every provider, contract and licence on one page. The redesign brought monthly IT cost from about €27,500 to €21,500, including new owned equipment spread over its life.

Monthly IT cost before~€27,500
Monthly IT cost after, including equipment~€21,500
Back into EBITDA each year~€75,000–90,000
Enterprise value at a 5× multiple~€350,000–450,000
Client example, converted from rand at roughly R20 to the euro. Investment in owned equipment was about €110,000. Estimates for illustration; your accountant confirms the treatment.

Every one of the four diligence areas can be built years in advance, and every one is a standard part of a properly run outsourced IT relationship: documented systems, evidenced controls, a clean asset register and reporting a buyer can read.

Chapter eight

What outsourced IT support and managed IT services should include.

Outsourced IT support means handing the running of your technology to an outside team instead of employing your own. Managed IT services is the industry's name for the same thing done properly: a fixed monthly fee for looking after your systems proactively, rather than paying by the hour when something breaks.

A good service covers:

  • Help when your people need it: a service desk that answers, in plain English, and owns the problem until it is fixed, including chasing the phone, internet and software suppliers for you.
  • Prevention: monitoring and updates for every device and server, so most problems are fixed before anyone notices.
  • Security: threat detection and response, email protection, multi-factor sign-in, staff awareness training and tested backups.
  • Strategy: a technology roadmap tied to where the business is going, reviewed with the owner, not just the office manager.
  • Reporting you can read: a monthly plain-English summary of what was done, what was found and what it means for the business.

Two models are common. Fully managed means the outside team runs everything. Co-managed means they work alongside your own IT person, filling gaps in security, out-of-hours cover or strategy. Either way, the price should be per user, all-inclusive, with no surprise invoices.

Chapter nine

How to choose an IT partner.

Most owners don't find their IT company on Google. They ask people they trust, get two or three names, then check whether each one is the right size. The website's job is to confirm what they've heard.

Size matters more than most owners expect. Too big, and you are a ticket number. Too small, and everything depends on one person who may not be there when you need them. The right partner is big enough for proper process and small enough that you know who answers.

The deciding factor is rarely price. It is whether they ask the right questions about your business, not just your systems. Five questions will tell you almost everything:

  • "How does your service affect our EBITDA?" A blank look means they manage computers, not value.
  • "Which of our workloads should we own, and which should we rent?" A good partner has an opinion, workload by workload.
  • "What would a buyer's technology diligence find tomorrow?" And can they show you evidence, not assurances?
  • "When something goes wrong, what happens in the first hour?" You want a process, not a promise that it won't.
  • "What happens if we want to leave?" No lock-in and a documented handover, or they are betting on how hard it is to switch.

That is the standard we built our service around, and the standard you should hold any provider to, including us.

Chapter ten

Five things you can do this quarter.

  • Put your IT spend on one page. Every subscription, licence and contract. Mark each line: does it protect value, build it, or just cost?
  • Ask the multiple question. Take your three biggest recurring technology costs and multiply each by 5. That is roughly what they cost you at sale.
  • Map your workloads. Which genuinely need to flex (rent) and which run steady (consider owning)? One page, honest answers.
  • Test one backup. Not "do we have backups?" Restore one file today, and time it.
  • Get an outside read. A second opinion on your security and spend costs nothing and anchors everything else.
