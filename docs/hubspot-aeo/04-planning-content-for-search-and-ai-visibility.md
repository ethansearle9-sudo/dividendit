## Video: Researching keywords for AEO content

Every piece of content you create starts with a question: what's your audience actually looking for? Keyword research has always been how marketers answer that question. But, as AI answer engines become a bigger part of how people find information, the way you approach keyword research needs to evolve too.

In an SEO world, keyword research was largely about finding high-volume search terms and optimizing your pages around them. In an AEO world, volume still matters, but it's no longer the whole story. AI engines are built to answer questions, not just match keywords. So your research needs to go deeper into the questions your audience is actually asking, the intent behind those questions, and the topics where your brand can genuinely be the best answer.

There's no single correct way to do keyword research. What follows is one practical approach you can adapt based on your business and your goals.

**The first step is to understand your audience.** Before you open any tool, have a clear understanding of who you're creating content for. What problems are they trying to solve? What questions do they ask at different stages of their journey? A marketer just learning about AEO has different needs than one who's ready to optimize existing content. Knowing your audience inside and out is what makes your keyword research meaningful rather than just mechanical.

**The second step is to identify your core topics.** Make a list of the subjects your business has the most expertise in and that your audience genuinely cares about. These become the foundation of everything else. If you run a B2B SaaS company, your core topics might include things like "pipeline management," "sales automation," or "customer onboarding." Don't try to cover everything at once. Pick three to five topics to start, and build from there.

**The third step is to find the questions people are asking.** This is where AEO keyword research diverges most from SEO. Instead of starting with keywords, start with questions. What's your audience trying to figure out within each of your core topics? Queries that begin with "how," "what," "why," "which," and "best way to" are strong signals of the kind of conversational intent AI engines are built to handle.

A few free tools can help you go deeper here. Google's "People Also Ask" section surfaces real questions your audience is actively searching for and is completely free to use. AnswerThePublic, a search listening and keyword research tool, offers a limited number of free searches per day and visualizes the full range of questions people ask around a topic, making it useful for broad brainstorming. AlsoAsked, another search tool, also offers free searches and is particularly helpful for understanding how questions relate to each other, which is valuable when building out content across a topic. And, Google Trends is free and helps you see whether interest in a topic is growing or declining over time, so you can prioritize accordingly.

Let's say your core topic is "customer onboarding." You could start by typing that phrase into Google and scrolling to the People Also Ask section. You might find questions like "what should be included in a customer onboarding plan?", "how long should customer onboarding take?", and "what are the stages of customer onboarding?" From there, you could take one of those questions into AnswerThePublic to uncover a broader set of related questions your audience is asking. Within a few minutes, you'd have a working list of real, intent-driven questions to build your content around.

**The fourth step is to take note of search volume and intent.** For each question you're considering, ask yourself two things. First, are enough people searching for this to make it worth creating content around? Not every keyword needs high volume, especially in AEO, where a highly specific question with a clear intent can be more valuable than a broad term with millions of searches. Second, what does the person asking this question actually want? Someone asking "what is pipeline management?" is in learning mode. Someone asking "how do I set up pipeline stages for a B2B sales team?" is ready to act. Mapping intent helps you prioritize which questions to answer first and what those answers should look like.

**The fifth step is to organize your questions into topic clusters.** Group related questions together around each of your core topics. This gives you a clear picture of the full range of questions your audience is asking within a subject area and helps you plan a body of content rather than individual pages. In AEO, topical depth matters. AI engines don't just evaluate a single page. They look at whether your brand covers a subject comprehensively.

Keyword research for AEO isn't about finding the biggest numbers. It's about understanding your audience well enough to anticipate what they're asking, and identifying the topics where your brand has the most opportunity to become the answer.

## Video: Reading the SERP to inform your content

You've done your keyword research. You have a list of questions your audience is asking. Now comes a step that a lot of marketers skip: actually looking at what's already showing up in search results before you write a single word.

The search engine results page, or SERP, tells you exactly what Google and AI engines consider to be the best answers to a given question right now. And, when you know how to read it, it becomes a roadmap for how to structure your own content. Let's discuss what to look for and how to use it.

Start by running the search. Take one of the questions from your keyword research and type it into Google exactly as your audience would ask it. Don't optimize it. Don't clean it up. Search the way a real person would. What you see on that results page is Google's current best judgment about what kind of content serves that query best.

Before you even click into individual results, take note of what kinds of content Google is surfacing at the top of the page. Is there a featured snippet, a short block of text pulled directly from a webpage, to answer the question? Is there a People Also Ask section with a cluster of related questions? Are there video results? Image packs? An AI Overview synthesizing information from multiple sources? Each of these features tells you something important about what format Google thinks best serves the intent behind that query.

If a featured snippet appears, Google has determined that this question has a single, direct answer. If videos dominate, the topic is better served visually. If an AI Overview appears, the question is one that AI engines are actively synthesizing answers for, which means your content needs to be structured for extraction, not just ranking.

From there, click into the first three to five pages ranking for your query and pay attention to three things. First, what format is the content in? Is it a step-by-step guide, a definition-led explainer, a comparison, or a short direct answer? If most of the top results follow the same format, that's a strong signal that the format matches what people want when they search for that topic. Second, how is the content structured? Look at the headings. Are they phrased as questions? Is the main answer placed at the top of the page, or buried further down? Third, how long and detailed is the content? Some topics are well served by a short, punchy answer. Others reward depth and comprehensive coverage. The results will tell you which one this topic calls for.

Pay close attention to the People Also Ask section too. It's a direct window into the follow-up questions your audience has after their initial search. These questions often point to gaps in existing content, subtopics worth covering, and the natural progression of how someone learns about a subject. If your content can address both the original question and several of the related People Also Ask questions, it becomes a much stronger candidate for AI citation because it demonstrates comprehensive topical coverage.

If an AI Overview appears for your query, take a close look at which sources are being cited. What do they have in common? Are they long-form or short? Do they use headers and lists, or flowing prose? Do they lead with a direct answer or build up to it? The sources Google's AI Overview selects are showing you exactly what structured, citable content looks like for that topic.

What you're doing through all of this is building a picture of what content already works for a given query. You're not copying what's there. You're understanding the format and structure your audience expects, and then creating something that meets that standard, while offering something more accurate, more current, or more useful.

Reading the SERP before you write isn't extra work. It's the step that makes everything else more effective. When your content matches what the results page is already telling you people want, it's far more likely to be found, cited, and trusted.

## Video: Creating content that works for both AI and search

You've identified your topics. You've read the search engine results page (SERP). Now it's time to put it all together into a content plan that doesn't just aim for rankings, but also positions your brand to show up in the AI-generated answers your audience is increasingly turning to first.

The good news is that these two goals aren't in conflict. Building content that earns traditional search rankings and content that gets cited by AI engines relies on the same foundation. The difference is in how deliberately you design for both.

Here's how to think about building that content plan.

**Start with your topic clusters.** A strong content plan isn't a list of individual pages. It's a set of topic areas where your brand is building genuine depth. For each core topic from your keyword research, map out the range of questions your audience is asking across the full journey, from someone just learning about the subject to someone ready to make a decision. AI engines don't just evaluate a single piece of content. They look at whether your brand has comprehensive, trustworthy coverage of a subject. A content plan built around topic depth serves both goals at once.

**For each piece of content in your plan, lead with the answer.** One of the clearest differences between content that gets cited by AI engines and content that doesn't is how quickly it gets to the point. AI systems are designed to extract direct, clear answers. If your main point is buried three paragraphs in, it's harder for both AI tools and human readers to find it. Plan each piece so the core answer to the question comes first, within the opening sentences, and then expands with context, examples, and supporting detail.

**Structure your content around questions.** When you plan your headings and sections, phrase them the way your audience actually asks them. A heading like "What's the difference between SEO and AEO?" is more useful to both readers and AI engines than "SEO vs. AEO Overview." This isn't just a formatting preference. It's how you align your content with the conversational queries that AI tools are built to handle. Plan for this at the outline stage, not as an afterthought when you're editing.

**Plan for depth, not just coverage.** A common mistake in content planning is spreading effort too thin, producing a large volume of short, surface-level pieces across many topics. For both SEO and AEO, depth signals authority. Your most-cited pages tend to be ones that answer multiple related questions in a single, comprehensive resource. Your content plan should include a mix of foundational, in-depth pieces on your most important topics and shorter, more specific pieces that address individual questions within those topics.

**Build freshness into your plan.** AI engines favor content that's accurate and current. A content plan that doesn't account for regular review and updates will gradually lose ground as information ages. Schedule time to revisit your most important pieces, refresh statistics, update examples, and check that the information still reflects current best practices. Freshness isn't just a nice-to-have. It's a signal that your brand is an active, reliable source.

**Finally, think beyond your own website.** AI engines don't just look at your site when deciding what to cite. They look at whether your brand is referenced, mentioned, and recommended across the web. A well-rounded content plan includes not just what you publish on your own channels, but also where your expertise shows up, through guest contributions, industry publications, or collaborations with other credible voices in your space. The more consistently your brand is referenced across the web, the stronger the signal to AI engines that you're a trustworthy, authoritative source worth citing.

### Worked example

Say you're a marketer at a B2B SaaS company and one of your core topics is customer onboarding. Here's what a content plan built around that topic might look like.

You start by mapping the questions your audience is asking across the full journey. Early-stage questions might include "what's customer onboarding?" and "why does customer onboarding matter?" Mid-stage questions might include "how do you build a customer onboarding process?" and "what should a customer onboarding checklist include?" Later-stage questions might include "how do you measure customer onboarding success?" and "what are common customer onboarding mistakes?"

From there, you plan a foundational, in-depth blog post that answers the most important question comprehensively — something like "what is customer onboarding and how does it work?" It should be structured so the core answer comes in the first paragraph and expands from there. You phrase your headings as questions your audience actually asks. You plan shorter supporting pieces that go deep on individual subtopics, like onboarding checklists or measuring time-to-value. You build a review cycle into the plan so the foundational piece gets refreshed every six months. And you identify two or three industry publications where your team could contribute guest articles on onboarding topics to build off-site visibility.

### Where to start

Pick one core topic your brand has genuine expertise in. Map out the questions your audience is asking across the full journey. Identify your most important foundational piece and plan it so the answer comes first. Build out supporting pieces that go deep on individual subtopics. Schedule a review cycle. And identify at least one or two places beyond your own website where your brand's expertise could show up.

That's a winning content plan designed to earn search rankings and AI citations at the same time, because every decision was made with both in mind from the start.

The goal of a content plan that works for both search and AI isn't to optimize for two separate algorithms. It's to create content that is genuinely useful, clearly structured, and consistently trustworthy — the kind of content that earns its place in search results and AI answers because it deserves to be there.
