Cyber insurance for small business: what it covers and what insurers ask for

Cyber insurance has changed. It used to be a box you ticked at renewal. Now insurers ask detailed questions about your security before they will quote, and the answers shape what you pay and whether a claim is paid at all. Here is what owners need to know.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·9 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. What cyber insurance is for
  2. Common gaps and exclusions
  3. The controls insurers now ask about
  4. How security changes what you pay
  5. How to buy it well

What cyber insurance is for

A cyber policy pays for the costs of a cyber incident that most business insurance does not. Wordings vary, but cover usually falls into two halves.

Your own costs (first party)

  • Incident response: the specialists who investigate, contain and clean up
  • Restoring data and systems
  • Loss of income while the business is disrupted
  • Extortion and ransom-related costs, where legal and insurable
  • Notifying affected people and handling the public response

Claims against you (third party)

  • Liability to customers or partners whose data was exposed
  • Legal defence costs
  • Regulatory investigation costs and, where the law allows, fines

For many smaller businesses, the most valuable part is not the money. It is the incident response team on the end of the phone when something goes wrong.

Common gaps and exclusions

Read the wording, or ask your broker these questions directly.

  • Is invoice fraud covered? Losses from being tricked into paying the wrong account often sit under a separate "social engineering" or "funds transfer fraud" section with a much lower limit, or are excluded entirely. See our invoice fraud guide.
  • What are the conditions? Many policies require prompt notice and the use of the insurer's approved responders. Calling your own IT company first and spending money without consent can affect a claim.
  • What did we declare? If the proposal form says multi-factor sign-in is enabled everywhere and it was not on the account that was breached, expect a difficult conversation.
  • Improvements and known issues. Policies generally will not pay to upgrade your systems beyond where they were, or for problems you knew about before cover started.
  • Waiting periods. Business interruption cover usually only starts after a set number of hours of disruption.

The controls insurers now ask about

Proposal forms differ, but the same handful of questions appear on almost all of them. Each one maps to a control that genuinely reduces risk.

Insurers askWhat it means in practice
Multi-factor authenticationOn email, remote access and admin accounts, without exceptions
Endpoint detection and responseModern protection on every device, ideally monitored around the clock
BackupsSeparate from the main network, protected from deletion, and tested
PatchingCritical updates applied within a defined time
Email securityFiltering, link protection and email authentication
Privileged accessFew admin accounts, separate from everyday accounts
TrainingRegular awareness training and phishing simulation
Incident response planWritten, with contacts, and tested

If you cannot answer "yes" honestly to these, fix that before renewal. It is better for the business and it is better for the conversation with your insurer.

How security changes what you pay

Security and insurance are two sides of the same risk. When you can show strong controls with evidence, rather than just ticking boxes, you widen the range of insurers willing to quote and put yourself in a stronger position on terms. When you cannot, cover can become more expensive, more restricted, or harder to find.

This is one of the clearest places where cyber security shows up in the financials. It is also something a buyer's advisers will look at: an insurable business with an evidenced security record is a lower-risk acquisition.

We help clients map their controls to the questions insurers ask and back each answer with evidence, so renewal is a short, accurate exercise and not a guessing game.

How to buy it well

  • Use a broker who specialises in cyber, and ask them to explain the exclusions in plain English.
  • Answer the proposal form with your IT provider in the room. Accuracy matters more than looking good.
  • Check the limit for social engineering and invoice fraud against the size of your typical payments.
  • Know the incident line and the conditions before you need them, and add them to your incident plan.
  • Review it each year as your business and your security change.

Frequently asked questions

Do small businesses need cyber insurance?

Most benefit from it, particularly those that hold customer data, rely on IT to trade or move significant payments by email. The most useful part is often the incident response support rather than the payout. It works best alongside good security, not instead of it.

What does cyber insurance not cover?

Common exclusions include improving systems beyond their previous state, known incidents before the policy started, and losses where the policy conditions were not met. Invoice fraud is often excluded or covered only at a low limit.

What security do I need for cyber insurance?

Insurers typically ask about multi-factor authentication, endpoint detection and response, protected and tested backups, patching, email security, control of admin accounts, staff training and an incident response plan.

Can a cyber insurance claim be refused?

Yes. Claims can be disputed if the proposal form was inaccurate, for example if multi-factor sign-in was declared but not enabled on a breached account, or if policy conditions such as prompt notification were not followed.
