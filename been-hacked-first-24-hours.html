Incident guide

We think we've been hacked. What do we do in the first 24 hours?

A plain-English plan for the owner or director who has just had that sinking feeling. Follow it in order. Most of the damage in a cyber incident is done by panic, delay and well-meant mistakes in the first few hours, not by the attacker.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·9 min read·Updated November 2026
If it is happening right now
  1. If money has left the account, call your bank's fraud line immediately. Minutes matter.
  2. Unplug affected computers from the network or switch off their WiFi, but leave them powered on.
  3. Call your IT or security provider. Then come back to this page.

First: three things not to do

Before you do anything, avoid the three mistakes that make incidents worse.

  • Do not switch everything off or wipe machines. It feels like the safe move, but it destroys the evidence that tells you how they got in and what they took. Disconnect from the network instead.
  • Do not use the system you think is compromised to talk about the incident. If a mailbox has been taken over, the attacker may be reading your emails about them. Use phones and a separate messaging app.
  • Do not pay anyone or agree to anything yet. Not a ransom, not a "recovery specialist" who contacts you out of the blue. You do not have enough information to make that decision.

The first hour: contain it

The goal of the first hour is simple: stop it getting worse.

  1. MoneyCall your bank if any payment might be affected. Ask for the fraud team, explain that a payment may have gone to a criminal account and ask them to attempt a recall. The faster the bank acts, the better the chance of getting money back.
  2. MachinesIsolate affected devices. Unplug the network cable or turn off WiFi on any computer that is behaving strangely or showing a ransom message. Leave it switched on.
  3. AccountsLock compromised accounts. From a device you trust, reset the password of any account you think has been taken over, sign it out of all sessions, and check that the multi-factor sign-in method has not been changed to the attacker's phone.
  4. PeopleCall your IT or security provider. Tell them what you saw, when, and what you have already done. If you do not have one, call your insurer's incident line first, because many policies provide responders.

Write down the time and what you see as you go: screenshots of ransom notes, the email that looked wrong, the account that was locked. A simple timeline is one of the most valuable things you can hand to responders, insurers and, if needed, the police.

Hours one to four: get the right people involved

Once the bleeding has stopped, put a small team around the problem.

  • One person in charge. Usually the owner or a director. They make the calls, approve spending and decide what to say to whom.
  • Your technical responders. Your IT partner or an incident response firm. If you have cyber insurance, check whether the policy requires you to use their approved responders. Using someone else can affect your cover.
  • Your insurer. Most cyber policies require prompt notice. Call the incident line on the policy, not just your broker.
  • Legal advice if personal data may be involved. Your insurer can often arrange this. It helps you get notifications right first time.

Agree how the team will communicate. A phone call at set times and a messaging group outside your normal systems works well.

Hours four to twelve: understand what happened

Now the responders work out the scope. As the owner, you need answers to four questions, in plain English:

  1. How did they get in? A phished password, an unpatched system, a supplier's access?
  2. What could they reach? One mailbox, the file server, the finance system, everything?
  3. What did they take or change? Data copied out, inbox rules created, bank details altered, backups deleted?
  4. Are they still in? And how will we know when they are gone?

Do not restore from backup until you know how they got in. If you restore systems with the same weakness still open, they will simply come back. This is the most common reason businesses get hit twice.

Hours twelve to twenty-four: notify and communicate

By the end of the first day you will need to decide who must be told. Take advice, but know the broad rules.

Regulators

If personal data may have been exposed, data protection law usually applies. Under GDPR in the EU and the UK, a reportable breach must be notified to the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. In South Africa, POPIA requires notification to the Information Regulator and affected people as soon as reasonably possible. The clock starts when you know, not when you finish investigating, so start early and update later.

Police and national cyber authorities

Report fraud and cyber crime to the police and, where one exists, your national cyber security centre. It helps them, and your bank and insurer will often ask for a reference number.

Customers and suppliers

If your email was taken over, the attacker may have sent fake invoices or bank details to people you do business with. Call your key customers and suppliers, by phone, and tell them not to act on any payment change that appears to come from you.

Your team

Tell staff what has happened, what to do and what not to do, and who to send questions to. Silence breeds rumours and mistakes.

If there is a ransom demand

Ransomware groups want you to feel you have no choice. You usually have more options than they let you believe.

  • Do not contact the criminals yourself. If negotiation is ever considered, it is done by specialists, with legal advice.
  • Paying does not guarantee you get your data back, or that copies are deleted. It also marks you as a business that pays.
  • Paying certain groups can breach sanctions law. That is a legal question, not a technical one.
  • If your backups are separate from your main systems and recently tested, you may be able to recover without engaging at all. This is exactly why we treat backup and recovery as a security control, not an IT chore.

After the first day: recover properly

Recovery is where good businesses separate themselves. Restore cleanly, from known-good backups, onto systems with the original weakness fixed. Then make the changes that stop it happening again, typically stronger sign-in protection, monitoring that someone actually watches, and a tested plan.

Finally, hold a short, blame-free review. What did we see, what did we do, what would we do differently? Write it down. If you are ever selling the business, a buyer will ask about past incidents. "Here is what happened, here is what we changed, here is the evidence" is a strong answer.

The one-page checklist

Print this and keep it somewhere you can find it when your systems are down.

  • Bank fraud line number: ______________________
  • IT or security provider out-of-hours number: ______________________
  • Cyber insurer incident line and policy number: ______________________
  • Who is in charge if the owner is away: ______________________
  • Disconnect affected devices; leave them powered on
  • Reset compromised accounts from a trusted device; check sign-in methods
  • Keep a timeline with times and screenshots
  • Do not restore until you know how they got in
  • Personal data involved? Take advice; 72-hour regulator clock under GDPR
  • Phone key customers and suppliers about any payment changes

Frequently asked questions

Should I turn off a computer I think has been hacked?

No. Disconnect it from the network by unplugging the cable or turning off WiFi, but leave it powered on. Switching it off can destroy evidence responders need to understand what happened.

Should we pay a ransom?

Not without specialist and legal advice. Payment does not guarantee recovery, may breach sanctions law depending on who is behind the attack, and marks you as a business that pays. Tested backups are what give you a real alternative.

Do we have to report a cyber attack?

If personal data may have been exposed, data protection law usually requires you to notify the regulator. Under GDPR that is within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to put people at risk. In-scope businesses under the EU NIS2 Directive have their own, shorter reporting deadlines. Take advice early.

Can the bank get our money back?

Sometimes, if you act fast. Banks can attempt to recall a payment or freeze funds at the receiving bank, but once money has been moved on, recovery becomes much harder. Call the fraud line the moment you suspect a problem.

Will our cyber insurance cover it?

It depends on the policy and on following its conditions, which often include notifying the insurer promptly and using its approved responders. Call the incident line on the policy as early as possible.
