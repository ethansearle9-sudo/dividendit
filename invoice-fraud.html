Invoice fraud and payment diversion: how it works and how to stop it

The most expensive cyber incident most smaller businesses will ever face is not ransomware. It is a real invoice, paid on time, into the wrong bank account. Here is exactly how it happens, why good people fall for it, and the handful of controls that stop it.

By Ethan Searle, Founder·10 min read·Updated November 2026

In this article

  1. What invoice fraud is
  2. How a typical attack unfolds
  3. Why it works on smart people
  4. Three versions to watch for
  5. The controls that stop it
  6. If you have already paid

What invoice fraud is

Invoice fraud, also called payment diversion, mandate fraud or business email compromise, is when a criminal persuades a business to send a legitimate payment to an account they control. There is usually no malware involved and nothing to see on your computers. The attack is on your process, through email.

It is also enormous. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded $2.77 billion in reported losses to business email compromise in 2024, from more than 21,000 complaints, and those are only the cases that were reported.

How a typical attack unfolds

  1. Week 1A password is stolen. Someone in your business, or at a supplier, enters their email password on a convincing fake login page.
  2. Weeks 1 to 4The attacker watches. They read the mailbox quietly, learn who pays whom, how invoices look, who approves payments and when large amounts are due. They often create an inbox rule to hide replies.
  3. The momentThe request arrives. Just before a large payment is due, an email arrives in the real thread, from the real address or a near-identical one: "Please note our bank details have changed. Updated letterhead attached."
  4. PaymentThe money goes. The invoice is genuine, the amount is expected, the timing is right. Finance updates the details and pays.
  5. Weeks laterThe supplier chases. They have not been paid. By now the money has been moved through several accounts and is very hard to recover.

Why it works on smart people

Owners often assume their team would never fall for it. The people who get caught are usually careful, conscientious and busy. The attack works because it removes every normal warning sign:

  • The email sits in a real conversation with a real supplier.
  • The invoice is genuine and the amount is expected.
  • The timing matches when payment is due.
  • There is often gentle pressure: a director copied in, or a reminder that the account will be on hold.

That is why the answer is not "tell people to be careful". It is a process that does not rely on anyone spotting the fake.

Three versions to watch for

Supplier impersonation

The attacker poses as a supplier and asks you to pay a new account. Their mailbox may be the compromised one, not yours, which is why your own security alone cannot stop it.

Director impersonation

An urgent email appears to come from the owner or finance director asking for a quick payment, a transfer to a new supplier or gift cards. It often arrives when the director is known to be travelling.

Your customers get targeted in your name

If your mailbox is compromised, the attacker can send your customers fake bank details from your real address. They pay the criminal, you are not paid, and the conversation about who is responsible is very uncomfortable.

The controls that stop it

Process controls (the ones that matter most)

  • Call-back verification. Any new payee or change of bank details is confirmed by phone, on a number taken from your own records, not from the email. No exceptions, regardless of who asks.
  • Two people for new payees and changed details. One person sets them up, a second approves.
  • Payee name checks. Use your bank's account name verification where available, such as Confirmation of Payee in the UK and Verification of Payee in the eurozone, and treat a mismatch as a stop sign.
  • A no-blame culture for pausing payments. Staff must know they will never be criticised for delaying a payment to check it.
  • Tell your customers how you will never contact them. A line on every invoice: "We will never change our bank details by email."

Technical controls

  • Multi-factor sign-in on every mailbox, so a stolen password is not enough. See identity and access management.
  • Alerts on suspicious inbox rules and forwarding, watched by someone who acts on them.
  • Email authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) set to enforce, so criminals cannot send email that appears to come from your exact domain.
  • Lookalike domain detection and external sender warnings, so an email from "dividend-it.co" instead of "dividendit.com" stands out.

If you have already paid

  1. Call your bank's fraud line now. Ask them to recall the payment and contact the receiving bank. Hours matter.
  2. Report it to the police and get a reference number.
  3. Check whose mailbox was compromised. Yours, the supplier's, or both. Look for inbox rules and recent sign-ins.
  4. Phone the genuine supplier or customer so they know what happened and can check their own systems.
  5. Tell your insurer. Some cyber and crime policies cover this kind of loss, often with a separate limit.

Then follow the wider plan in what to do in the first 24 hours.

Frequently asked questions

What is business email compromise?

Business email compromise is fraud where criminals use a hacked or impersonated email account to trick a business into sending money or sensitive information. Invoice fraud and payment diversion are the most common forms.

How do I know if an email asking to change bank details is fraudulent?

Assume any request to change bank details could be fraudulent, however genuine it looks. Confirm it by phone on a number you already had before the email arrived. If the email discourages you from calling, treat that as a red flag.

Can the bank recover money lost to invoice fraud?

Sometimes, if the fraud is reported quickly. Banks can try to recall the payment or freeze funds at the receiving bank, but once the money has been moved on, recovery is unlikely. Speed is everything.

Does cyber insurance cover invoice fraud?

Some policies do, often under a social engineering or funds transfer fraud section with a lower limit than the main policy. Many do not cover it by default. Check your wording with your broker.
